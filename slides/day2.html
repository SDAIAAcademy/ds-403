Review of Day 1

Which standard error belongs in the report?

A new task set has 400 questions from 40 documents. The naive standard error of a configuration's score is 0.020 and the clustered one is 0.032. Which should the report use, and what does it imply?

The naive one: it is more precise
The mean of the two, 0.026
Either, if each question was sampled five times
The clustered one: the 400 questions are worth about 156 independent ones
Why. Questions from one document share its difficulty; DEFF = (0.032 / 0.020)² = 2.56, so 400 / 2.56 ≈ 156.

Answer: D. Ask someone to compute the design effect aloud. If the room hesitates, go back to the Day 1 table of design effects (2.4 to 2.9 for the four configurations).

The design you already have

C and D answered the same 600 questions, so compare them question by question

Unpaired

Two scores, two standard errors, combined as if the question sets were different.

Paired

One difference per question, D minus C, and the standard error of that one column.

Same mean, different width. Both estimate D minus C = 2.2 points; only the paired analysis uses the fact that the questions are shared.

Recap from AE-303, which compared two configurations with a paired bootstrap. Here the analytic version, clustered, and the reason it is narrower. Miller's paper recommends paired differences whenever two models answer the same questions.

Sources: arXiv 2411.00640.

Why pairing helps

Hard questions are hard for both, and the difference removes what they share

Var(D − C) = Var(D) + Var(C) − 2 Cov(C, D)

Unpaired analysis drops the covariance term. With a strong positive correlation it overstates the variance of the difference several times over.

0.83
correlation of C's and D's per-question scores

From Lab 2.1. Ask: what would the correlation be if C and D were two unrelated systems? Near zero, and pairing would buy nothing. Models evaluated on the same items are almost never unrelated: the question's difficulty dominates.

Lab 2.1 result

Pairing makes the interval four times narrower than the clustered unpaired one

Analysis of D minus CSE95% half-width
Unpaired, naive0.0215±4.2 points
Unpaired, clustered0.0338±6.6 points

Two lessons in one table. Clustering widens an unpaired comparison (row 2 against row 1). Once paired, clustering hardly matters (row 4 against row 3), because each document's effect moves C and D together and cancels in the difference. Always report the clustered paired version: it costs nothing and protects against the case where it does matter.

The calculation

One column of differences, one clustered standard error

t = U.paired_table("C", "D", 0.2)     # one row per question
d = t["diff"].mean()                  # 0.0217
se = U.clustered_se(t["diff"], t.doc_id)   # 0.0086
lo, hi = d - 1.96 * se, d + 1.96 * se      # 0.005 to 0.039

Point out that the per-question score is already the mean of five samples: the pairing is at the level of questions, the sampling noise is inside each question's mean. A paired bootstrap over documents gives the same width within rounding; the stretch of Lab 2.2 does that for ranks.

The verdict against the minimum effect

D is better than C, but not shown to be better by three points

Paired, clustered by document; 600 questions, five samples at temperature 0.2 (Lab 2.1).

The plan's middle branch: the lower end is above zero, so D is better; the interval includes values below three, so "better by enough to switch" is not shown.

Do not let the room round this up to "D wins". Day 4 adds a reason why even the 2.2 is suspect.

The decision note

Three lines the decision owner can act on

Paired difference D minus C: 2.2 points, 95% CI 0.5 to 3.9, clustered by document, 600 questions, 5 samples, temperature 0.2. Minimum effect: 3 points. Verdict: better, not shown to matter. The unpaired analysis would have given ±6.6 points and no conclusion.
  • At temperature 0.8 the difference is 2.9 points, CI 0.8 to 5.0: the same verdict.

This is the evidence for LO2: a decision note with the paired difference, its interval and a verdict. The last sentence is optional in a real note but useful here: it shows the reader what the design bought.

Checkpoint

Two configurations on the same questions

Two configurations answer the same 500 questions. Their scores' intervals overlap heavily. A colleague concludes there is no difference. What is wrong with that?

Overlapping separate intervals ignore the correlation; the paired interval of the difference can exclude zero
The intervals should have been clustered first

Family-wise error

Adjust the threshold to the number of comparisons you make

MethodRulePairs separated

Both adjustments keep the chance of any false separation among the fifteen at 5%. Holm never separates fewer pairs than Bonferroni.

Recap from DS-302, where participants corrected twenty segment results for false discoveries. Here the family-wise rate is the right target: a release decision should not rest on a single false separation. False discovery rate control (Benjamini and Hochberg) is the alternative when many comparisons are screened.

Checkpoint

Announcing a winner

Of six configurations, F has the highest pass rate. After Holm adjustment F is separated from A, B and C, but not from D or E. What may the release note say?

F is the best configuration
F is the best, because it beats D at the 25% level
F, D and E are tied at the top; F is better than A, B and C
F, D and E are shown to be equally good
Why. The adjusted analysis separates F from three configurations only; between F, D and E the data cannot tell, which is a tie in the evidence, not a proof of equality.

Answer: C. Option D is the subtle trap: absence of a separation is not evidence of equivalence. An equivalence claim needs its own margin and test.

The Holm loop is where most errors happen: comparing with alpha over k instead of alpha over (m minus k), or not stopping at the first failure. The rank logic trips on the sign: the difference is b minus a, so a positive difference means b is better.

Before the next evaluation

Size the evaluation for the difference that matters, not for the one you hope to see

The effect

Three points, from Dr. Nadeen: the minimum worth a switch.

The errors

5% false alarm, 80% power: a true three-point gain is found four times in five.

The noise

The spread of per-question differences, from a pilot.

The 2027 regulations need a new task set. Today's C against D comparison is the pilot. The pilot's observed difference (2.2 points) plays no role in the sizing: using it would size for whatever the pilot happened to show.

The sizing plan

Three lines that go into the committed plan

Detect D minus C = 3 points (paired, two-sided, alpha 0.05) with 80% power: 417 questions from about 56 documents, 5 samples each at temperature 0.2. Pilot: 600 questions of the 2026 set; SD of the per-question difference 0.219; design effect 0.93; simulated power at 417 questions 85%.

This is the evidence for LO3 (a sizing plan with power by formula, checked by simulation). The capstone asks for the same three lines for the participant's own configurations.

Checkpoint

Sizing from a pilot

A pilot of two configurations on 200 shared questions shows a 6-point difference and an SD of per-question differences of 0.25. The decision needs to detect 3 points. Which inputs should the sizing use?

The 6-point difference and SD 0.25
The 3-point minimum effect and SD 0.25
3 points and the SD of each configuration's scores
200 questions, since the pilot already found a difference
Why. The effect to detect comes from the decision, the noise from the pilot; the pilot's observed difference is itself uncertain and would undersize the study.

Answer: B. Ask: with 6 points the formula gives a quarter of the questions; what would happen if the true difference were 3? Power would be well under 50%.

Watch for: using the variance of the scores instead of the differences; centring the resampled differences on zero instead of three points (power then equals the false alarm rate, about 5%: a good diagnostic). The simulation takes about a minute in full mode.

The trade-off

The variance falls with questions and samples; the cost rises with both

Variance

(between + within / K) / n

Cost

n × (15 + 0.10 K) ≤ 6,000

Minimising the first under the second gives K* = √((15 / 0.10) × within / between).

The variance parts come from Lab 1.3 (D: between 0.123 and within 0.068 at temperature 0.2; 0.087 and 0.124 at 0.8). This ignores clustering by document: the next slide but one says what that changes.

Questions cost 150 samples each, so several samples per question pay; more of them pay at 0.8, where sampling noise is larger.

The flat optimum is the practical message: any K from 5 to 15 is within a few percent. What matters more is not to spend everything on one end: K = 1 or K = 30 are both clearly worse.

What the budget must include

Leave the grader out of the cost and you over-sample; leave people out and you cannot correct

Grader call forgotten

K* at 0.2 rises from 9.1 to 14.4: the plan buys samples it cannot afford to grade.

Human labels reserved

SAR 1,200 for 300 labels leaves 300 questions × 10: SE 0.0208 instead of 0.0186.

The reserve for human labels is the stretch of Lab 2.4 and the bridge to Day 3: without labels the grader's error cannot be measured or corrected. A slightly wider interval that is unbiased beats a narrow one that is wrong.

One more term

With documents in the picture, new questions should come from new documents

  • The between-item part is inflated by the design effect, about 2.4 for one score.
  • That favours spending on questions over samples, and on documents over questions per document.
  • A full model has three costs: a document, a question, a sample.

Ask the room to sketch the three-level version: documents, questions per document, samples per question, each with its variance component and its cost. The capstone does not require it, but strong capstones use it.
