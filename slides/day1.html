The frame

An evaluation is an experiment, and its questions are a sample

Populationevery question applicants could ask about the regulations
→
Samplethe 600 questions someone wrote
→
EstimateD passes 74.7% of them
The question a reader cares about is how D would do on questions not yet written. The 74.7% is an estimate of that, with a standard error.

This frame is from Evan Miller's paper on error bars for evaluations: questions as draws from an unseen super-population. Ask: "If Khattab had written a different 600 questions, would D still score 74.7%?" No: so the score has sampling error.

Sources: arXiv 2411.00640.

The formula

A pass rate is a mean of zeros and ones, so it has a standard error

±1.8
points: the standard error of D's first sample, 600 questions
  • SE = √(p(1 − p) / n) with p = 0.747 and n = 600 gives 0.0178.
  • A 95% interval is p ± 1.96 SE: 71.2% to 78.1%.
  • Four times the questions halves the standard error.

Reading an interval

The interval is about new questions, not about these 600

It says

If questions were written again the same way, intervals built like this would contain D's true pass rate 95% of the time.

It does not say

That D passes between 71.2% and 78.1% of these 600 questions: on these, the rate is known exactly.

And it is silent on questions of a different kind: dialect, new regulations, other agencies.

The last line foreshadows Day 4: an interval only covers the population the items were drawn from. The 2027 regulations are a different population.

Reporting

A score travels with its interval, its n, its samples and its temperature

D: 74.7% (95% CI 71.2 to 78.1), 600 questions, 1 sample each, temperature 0.2
  • Without the interval, a one-point lead looks like a win.
  • Without n, samples and temperature, nobody can reproduce or compare it.

This is the line participants write at the end of Lab 1.1. On Day 4 it grows into the reporting standard. Ask: which of the five parts is most often missing in reports you have read? Usually the samples and the temperature.

Checkpoint

What does the interval describe?

Configuration B scores 69.8% on the 600 questions, 95% interval 66.7% to 72.9%. A manager asks what the interval means. Which answer is right?

B passes between 66.7% and 72.9% of these 600 questions
B's pass rate on questions written the same way is plausibly between 66.7% and 72.9%
Each answer of B passes with a probability between 66.7% and 72.9%
B will score between 66.7% and 72.9% on next year's regulations
Why. The interval estimates the pass rate on the population the questions were sampled from; on the sample itself there is no uncertainty, and other populations are not covered.

Answer: B. If many choose A, return to the slide "The interval is about new questions". If many choose D, say: the 2027 regulations are a different population; Day 4 audits exactly that kind of claim.

Walk the room. Typical problems: forgetting to keep only sample 1 (they get 3,000 rows and a too-small SE); filtering temperature with == on floats (use np.isclose); bootstrapping indices once instead of B times. Anyone who finishes early does the stretch: the document bootstrap.

How the items were written

Questions written from one document succeed and fail together

  • Khattab's team wrote 4 to 9 questions from each of 80 regulation documents.
  • A document the model reads well yields many passes; an obscure one, many failures.
  • So the 600 questions are not 600 independent draws: they are 80 clusters.

What it does

Formulas that assume independence understate the standard error. The interval is too narrow and differences look more certain than they are.

Ask the room where else items cluster in evaluations they know: reading-comprehension sets with several questions per passage, code benchmarks with several tests per repository, multi-turn tasks per conversation. Miller recommends clustered standard errors for exactly this.

Sources: arXiv 2411.00640.

The clustered standard error

Sum the deviations inside each document before squaring

def clustered_se(x, groups):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    sums = pd.Series(x - x.mean()).groupby(groups).sum()
    return np.sqrt((sums ** 2).sum()) / len(x)

Deviations of the same sign inside one document add up, so a document whose questions fail together contributes much more than nine independent questions would.

Write the formula on the board: SE_cl = (1/n) times the square root of the sum over documents of the squared sum of deviations. With one question per document it reduces to the naive formula (up to n versus n minus 1).

Design effect

The design effect says how many independent questions the 600 are worth

  • DEFF = (SE clustered / SE naive)²
  • Effective number of questions: n / DEFF
  • D: naive SE 0.0151, clustered 0.0234
2.4
D's design effect: 600 questions are worth about 249 independent ones

From Lab 1.2's run on the mean of five samples at temperature 0.2. The interval is the square root of 2.4, about 1.55 times wider than the naive one.

Numbers from Lab 1.2. statsmodels applies a small-sample factor of G over G minus 1; with 80 documents it adds 0.6%. The stretch item clusters by category: 8 clusters is too few for the sandwich estimator to be trusted without a t correction.

This anticipates Day 2's sizing. The rule of thumb: when DEFF is large, add clusters, not items per cluster. Ask: how many documents does your own evaluation set draw from?

Two sources of noise

A score varies because of which questions were written and because the model samples

Between items

Some questions are easy, some hard. Only more questions reduce this part.

Within items

Asked twice, the model may answer differently. More samples per question reduce this part.

Var(question mean) = between + within / K for the mean of K samples per question.

Miller's paper recommends resampling each question several times when the model is sampled with a nonzero temperature: it removes part of the noise for free compared with writing questions. Today's lab measures how big that part is.

Temperature is part of the design

Fix the temperature and the samples in the plan, not after the results

  • Evaluate at the temperature the deployed configuration will use.
  • At temperature 0, sampling variance is near zero, but the score still has between-item error.
  • Report the samples per question and how they were combined.

Watad will run at 0.2 in production, so 0.2 is the primary analysis; 0.8 is a robustness check. Some serving stacks are not exactly deterministic at temperature 0; say so in the plan rather than assuming zero within-item variance.

Checkpoint

Twenty samples per question

At temperature 0.2, D's between-item variance is 0.123 and its within-item variance 0.068. A colleague proposes 20 samples per question in place of 5 to "make the score precise". What happens?

The standard error barely falls, because the between-item part does not shrink with samples
The standard error halves, as it would with four times the questions
The pass rate rises, because more samples find more right answers
The score becomes pass@20, which is the right metric at last
Why. More samples reduce only the within / K term; the between-item term, 0.123 here, stays, so the SE goes from 0.0151 to 0.0145.

Answer: A. Ask a participant to compute the two SEs aloud from the table on the previous slides.

Typical problems: using ddof=0 for the within-item variance (biases it down by a fifth with K = 5); forgetting to subtract within over K from the variance of means. If anyone gets a negative between-item variance, they subtracted within, not within over K.

The minimum effect

A difference can be real and still too small to act on

  • Switching configurations costs migration, retesting and serving.
  • Dr. Nadeen will switch only for three points or more.
  • Written before the results, the threshold cannot drift to fit them.

Three verdicts

Worth switching; real but too small to matter; not shown. The plan says which interval leads to which.

The minimum effect also drives the sizing on Day 2: the smaller the effect that matters, the more questions are needed. Ask the decision owner for it, do not invent it.

The decision rule

Write what each possible interval leads to, before you see it

Whole interval above 3 pointsswitch to D
↓
Above 0, but includes values below 3D is better; whether by enough is not shown
↓

The middle branch is the uncomfortable one and the most common. Agree with the decision owner in advance what happens there: often, keep the cheaper configuration or run a larger evaluation sized on Day 2.
